# Let code decide what the LLM proposes

**Short answer:** the best published results come from pipelines that break the work into small steps. Each step gets a narrow, section-scoped input. The LLM writes a proposal with an exact quote or cell reference, and plain code checks it before anything is accepted. On structured inputs like yours, LLMs are already good at numbers. When a GPT-4o-class model reads table source with a JSON schema, it gets **98.8% of result values right** in a small human audit ([LLMEvalDB](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.18791v2)). They are weaker on typed relations. Prompted LLMs reach only **about 38–41 strict relation F1 on SciER, against 61–66 for supervised or RL-tuned models** ([SciER](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.21155v1); [MimicSFT/R²GRPO](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2505.22068)). Fine-grained citation types are also hard. **GPT-4 zero-shot scores 38.6 macro-F1 on the 6-class ACL-ARC scheme, against 75.6 for fine-tuned SciBERT** ([Shui et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2410.13332)). The closest published match to your lineage design is Intern-Atlas. Its production model types edges at **70.4% accuracy, against 93.0% for a Claude Sonnet audit model**, and code drops any edge whose quote is not a real substring of the paper ([Intern-Atlas](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.28158)). A mini-class model comes within **about 2 F1 of its frontier sibling at 5–10x lower cost** ([arXiv 2510.01235](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.01235v1)). Two models from different families that agree are right **about 94% of the time; when they disagree, about 41–50%** ([medRxiv 2024](https://www.medrxiv.org/content/10.1101/2024.09.20.24314108.full.pdf)). So the design that fits your pipeline is: a cheap model proposes, code checks quotes and cells, and a stronger model or a human sees only the disagreements. Nobody has published a benchmark for a full "paper card" or for typed lineage edges. Most model rankings below are from 2024–2025 model generations. That means your own gold set is both required and a thesis-worthy contribution.

## Paper cards: entities are easy, relations and limitations are not

For the "problem, method, datasets, metrics" fields, the useful benchmark is SciER. It has 106 full-text ML papers with Method/Task/Dataset entities and 9 relation types. The supervised HGERE model scores **NER 86.85 and strict relation F1 61.10** in-distribution. Prompted LLMs trail by about 20 points: Qwen2-72B reaches strict relation F1 41.22, and Llama3-70B reaches NER 76.02 ([SciER](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.21155v1)). Newer zero-shot results tell the same story. **Gemini 2.0 scores NER 69.85 and relation F1 38.38. DeepSeek-R1 scores only 60.27 and 27.98**, and the "thinking" variant of Gemini 2.0 scored *lower* than the plain one (61.43 / 32.30) ([MimicSFT/R²GRPO](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2505.22068)). The one approach that closes the gap is a LoRA-tuned Qwen2.5-7B trained with SFT plus a reinforcement-learning reward. It reaches **relation F1 66.81 in-distribution and 55.08 out-of-distribution**. Plain SFT, by contrast, scored 41.01 in-distribution but **collapsed to 18.12 strict relation F1** on out-of-distribution papers ([MimicSFT/R²GRPO](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2505.22068)). That collapse matters to you: a model tuned on NLP papers can quietly fail on robotics or multi-agent papers. The result is still a preprint, and its supervised baselines were copied from SciER rather than re-run.

Three design lessons from SciER apply directly to your card. Run the steps as a pipeline, finding entities first and then relations. Pipeline prompting beat joint prompting, **41.22 vs 34.87**. More than 60% of candidate entity pairs have no relation, and **LLMs over-assign relation types** to them. Removing the annotation guidelines from the prompt hurts results ([SciER](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.21155v1)). SciER also found that tagging entity spans inline with HTML-style markers cut surface-form drift, such as the model writing "CNN" when the text said "CNNs" ([SciER](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.21155v1)). Your arXiv HTML input is well suited to that trick.

The card needs a role on every dataset and method: *used*, *proposed*, *compared*, or *merely mentioned*. ChatPD found that LLMs and rule-based systems both pick up datasets named only in Related Work ([ChatPD](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2505.22349)). The same paper gives the best cost reference for this field. GPT-4o-mini extracted dataset information at **F1 0.872 for $0.00063 per paper on a 1.5k-token excerpt, against 0.904 for $0.00447 on the full paper**. So you keep about 96% of the quality for roughly 1/7 of the cost ([ChatPD](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2505.22349)). Linking extracted names to canonical entries worked best with a graph built from names and URL identifiers: **F1 0.883, against 0.568 for plain name matching** ([ChatPD](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2505.22349)). No source measured how accurately *method* names are linked, or how well models tell versions apart (e.g. "Llama 2" vs "Llama 2 7B").

Treat limitations as two separate fields. **Author-stated limitations can be extracted well.** BAGELS used a section parser, a regex for implicit mentions and a GPT-4o-mini filter, and scored **85.69 F1 on ACL and 72.42 on NeurIPS** against human gold. Over 90% of sampled items were grounded in the source. Llama 3 70B was "unsatisfactory" at the same job ([BAGELS](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.18207v2)). *Generating* limitations the authors did not write is far noisier, with coverage F1 between 0.24 and 0.67 depending on model and retrieval ([BAGELS](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.18207v2)). Only author-stated limitations, each backed by a quote, belong on a canonical card.

Released artifacts are the field where LLMs are weakest and code is strongest. On MOLE's metadata task, links and licences were the hardest attributes, because they often sit outside the paper. Overall scores spread narrowly across models: **Gemini 2.5 Pro 67.42, GPT-4o 66.68, Claude 3.5 Sonnet 65.37, Gemma 3 27B 60.30**. About 5% of API calls failed, mostly on JSON parsing ([MOLE](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.19800v1)). Pull code, model and data links with a URL regex over the text and footnotes, then resolve them with GitHub and Hugging Face lookups. The LLM should only label what each link is. The "problem" and "method components" fields have no benchmark at all. The nearest one, ORKG property extraction, found that LLM dimensions matched expert schemas only **41.2%** of the time, though 65.2% of experts found the suggestions useful ([ORKG LLM eval](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.02105v1)).

## Lineage edges: fine-grained citation types sit near the human ceiling

Your eight edge types are a harder version of the classic citation-intent task, and the classic numbers are sobering. On ACL-ARC, the 6-class scheme that includes "Extension", **GPT-4 scores 38.55 macro-F1 zero-shot and 50.18 five-shot. Multi-task fine-tuned SciBERT reaches 75.57** ([Shui et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2410.13332)). The best open prompted model, Qwen2.5-14B, reaches **65.64 few-shot on ACL-ARC and 80.41 on the coarser 3-class SciCite**. LoRA fine-tuning lifts it to 68.48 and 86.84 ([Koloveas et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.14561v2)). A July 2026 preprint got **76.35 on ACL-ARC from a fine-tuned Falcon-7B** that scored only 1.38 zero-shot. Fine-tuning, not model size, drives these scores ([Vodička et al. 2026](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2607.17738.md)). No source reports Claude 4.x or GPT-5 on these benchmarks. ACL-ARC's test set has only 139 citations, so differences of ±5 points are noise.

Your most valuable edge types are also the rarest. In ACL-ARC, **Extension is 3.7% of citations, Motivation 5.0% and Future 3.5%** ([Vodička et al. 2026](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2607.17738.md)). The best 2019 model reached Extension F1 66.7, and per-class scores swing between roughly 36 and 75 across model variants ([Cohan et al. 2019](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1904.01608)). Contradiction is rarer still. The 2026 AC³ dataset has **7 Contradiction and 9 Criticizing citations out of 530**, and prompted 7–8B LLMs almost never predicted those labels (6–22 macro-F1 overall) ([Vodička et al. 2026](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2607.17738.md)). Expect `refutes_or_contradicts` to have poor recall unless you add a dedicated high-recall pass and verify it separately. No source gives per-class F1 for any LLM.

Human agreement sets the ceiling. For 6–7 class schemes, MultiCite label agreement is 0.70–0.76 ([MultiCite](https://preview.aclanthology.org/fix_video/2022.naacl-main.137.pdf)) and AC³ reaches kappa 0.71 ([Vodička et al. 2026](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2607.17738.md)). Experts agree 93.9% on the binary "important vs incidental" question and 83.6% on four classes ([Valenzuela et al. 2015](https://ai2-website.s3.amazonaws.com/publications/ValenzuelaHaMeaningfulCitations.pdf)). Nobody has measured agreement on distinctions like "extends vs improves vs replaces a component". A ~70% LLM accuracy on your 8-way scheme is therefore probably near what two careful humans would agree on. Your gold set should record inter-annotator agreement before you judge any model against it.

How much context to give depends on the level you classify at. A cited paper is mentioned **about 9.1 times per citing paper and carries 3.8 distinct functions on average**. About 19% of citations have more than one label, and 17% of contexts span more than one sentence ([MultiCite](https://preview.aclanthology.org/fix_video/2022.naacl-main.137.pdf)). For classifying a single mention, a ±1-sentence window was best among fixed windows. Wider windows degraded steadily, from 0.62 to 0.46 strict accuracy at window 9. The true variable-length context did best (0.66), and on 2-sentence contexts it raised strict accuracy from 0.28 to 0.46 ([MultiCite](https://preview.aclanthology.org/fix_video/2022.naacl-main.137.pdf)). Whole paragraphs hurt 7–8B models ([Vodička et al. 2026](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2607.17738.md)). Telling the model which section a citation sits in raised "Uses" F1 from 65.4 to 75.0 ([Cohan et al. 2019](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1904.01608)). Your parser already has section labels, so this signal costs nothing.

The 2026 lineage systems show what works at scale. **Intern-Atlas** is the closest analogue to your design. It builds 9.4M typed edges among 1.03M AI papers using seven types ordered by causal strength (extends, improves, replaces, adapts, uses_component, compares, background). Every non-background edge carries quoted bottleneck, mechanism and trade-off spans. The citing paper contributes only its Introduction, Method and Related Work, and the cited paper only its title and abstract. A high-recall first pass types every reference, about 21.2 per paper. A **deterministic post-checker then drops edges whose quotes fail substring match, that break publication-year order, or that conflict with an opposite-direction edge** ([Intern-Atlas](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.28158)). It is a preprint, it reports no precision/recall or human agreement, and its 70.4%-vs-93.0% gap rests on its own audit model. **THE-Tree** checks each proposed link with retrieval plus a fine-tuned DeBERTa NLI model, falling back to Qwen2.5-72B on neutral cases. It reaches relation P/R/F1 of 0.64/0.78/0.70 against expert trees ([THE-Tree](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2506.21763)). **Sci-Reasoning** has GPT-5 read full papers and pick 5–10 predecessors with relations like "extends" and "combines with". Recall is **89.73% for GPT-5 vs 68.53% for GPT-5-mini**, but precision is not reported ([Sci-Reasoning](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2601.04577)). That gap is a rare data point where the frontier model clearly earns its cost.

Your eight types map onto these schemes. `extends` matches Intern-Atlas extends/improves and ACL-ARC Extension. `combines` matches Sci-Reasoning's "combines with". `replaces_component` matches Intern-Atlas replaces and uses_component. `transplants_domain` matches Intern-Atlas "adapts" and Sci-Reasoning's "bridges"; that paper found "Cross-Domain Synthesis" behind **18.0%** of top-venue papers ([Sci-Reasoning](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2601.04577)). `refutes_or_contradicts` matches AC³ Contradiction and MultiCite "Differences". Two of your types do not need an LLM to decide. `evaluates_on` and `compares_against` show up in the results table itself: a benchmark column whose header links to a citation, or a baseline row whose model name carries a citation. AxCell's cell typing already separated "paper model" from "cited model" cells ([AxCell](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2004.14356)). Let code derive those two edges from the verified results rows, and spend LLM effort on the five types that need reading.

## Results rows: copying cells is solved, assigning attributes is not

Your arXiv HTML table cells remove the main reason older leaderboard extractors failed. When models read prose or RAG chunks, the score was always the weakest field. In the SOTA?/Kabongo line, fine-tuned 7B models scored **single-digit exact F1 on scores**, and fell to **0.63 overall exact F1 when fed the full paper** ([arXiv 2407.02409](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.02409v1)). In SciLead, GPT-4-Turbo got the (task, dataset, metric) triple at 72.75 F1 but the full tuple with result at only 55.27. The result errors came from the wrong table (33%), appendix results (31%) and misses (36%) ([SciLead](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.12656v1)). When models read the table source directly, the picture flips. **INSTRUCTE** feeds the table's source code, a JSON schema and BM25-retrieved paragraphs, and asks for one JSON record per cell. GPT-4 reaches **74.2 table-F1 on ML tables (human 96.1)**, and LaTeX vs HTML input made no difference (74.2 vs 74.1) ([Bai et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.14336)). **LLMEvalDB** (ACL 2025) used GPT-4o with a schema of value, dataset, subset, model, metric, prompting method and shot count. A two-annotator audit scored **metric value 98.8%, metric 100%, model 100%, dataset 95%, shots 95%, prompting method 86.3%** ([LLMEvalDB](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.18791v2)). The audit covered only 40 records, so read 98.8% as "very high", not as an exact figure.

The remaining errors are in *which row means what*, not in the digits. In INSTRUCTE's GPT-4 error analysis, about a third of attribute errors needed main-text context: 19.5% predicted the table header when the answer was in the text, and 14.9% missed text-only information. Removing retrieved paragraphs cost about 3 F1. **Removing the caption *improved* F1 from 72.3 to about 75.5**, because vague captions misled the model ([Bai et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.14336)). The context recipe is the table cells, the paragraphs that reference "Table N", and the experimental-setup section. Include the caption as one input among several, not as ground truth.

Agent-specific settings (harness, scaffold, attempts/pass@k, base model) are a published blank. **No paper measured extraction accuracy for scaffold, attempts or harness fields.** The closest proxies are LLMEvalDB's prompting method (86.3%) and shot count (95%). Those fields were also often simply absent: shots were missing in **9,081 of 18,127 records** and prompting method in 5,489 ([LLMEvalDB](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.18791v2)). Make every setting field nullable with an explicit "not stated", and look for settings in captions, footnotes and the setup section. A row whose harness is unknown should not be compared on a leaderboard with one whose harness is known.

Normalization should run off a curated alias table. SciLead's fully pre-defined taxonomy scored **55.27 vs 40.07** when new entities were added on the fly ([SciLead](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.12656v1)). LLMEvalDB abandoned LLM clustering of dataset names because it produced too many false positives and used rule-based matching instead ([LLMEvalDB](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.18791v2)). A 2025 position paper adds a reporting warning: micro vs macro averaging moved recall by **12.61 points** in one replication. Most systems also keep only the top result per paper, which throws away baselines and negative results ([Timmer et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.17465)). Keep every row, and record the averaging rule you use.

Your planned check, that each value appears in the cited cell, is **stricter than anything published**. The closest published gate is INSTRUCTE's rule-based checker, which truncates and re-prompts when generated records stop following the table's cell order. That cut cost from about $670 to $100 at similar accuracy ([Bai et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.14336)). With cell IDs in the prompt and an exact match on value (after stripping bold, ±, and % scaling), value hallucination should approach zero. Attribute errors, such as the wrong split or wrong setting, need a second check: the claimed benchmark and metric should match the cell's column and row header path.

## Shared choices: model tier, context, output format, verification, cost, distillation

**Model tier.** Pick by sub-task, not globally. On structured extraction from full-text papers, **GPT-4.1 scored 0.909 micro-F1 and GPT-4.1-Mini 0.889**, with Mini estimated at 5–10x cheaper. Gemini 2.0 Flash held precision (0.903) but lost recall (0.695) ([arXiv 2510.01235](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.01235v1)). Cheap models fail by *missing* things, so a precision-only audit flatters them. Measure recall against gold. The frontier tier earns its cost on judgment-heavy edges: predecessor recall was **89.73% (GPT-5) vs 68.53% (GPT-5-mini)** ([Sci-Reasoning](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2601.04577)), and lineage typing was **93.0% vs 70.4%** in Intern-Atlas ([Intern-Atlas](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.28158)). Small open models without fine-tuning are not viable for full papers. Prompted Qwen2.5-7B scored **7.09 relation F1** on SciER ([MimicSFT/R²GRPO](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2505.22068)), and 7B fine-tunes collapse on full text ([arXiv 2407.02409](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.02409v1)). **Reasoning mode has no evidence of helping span extraction.** DeepSeek-R1 trailed Gemini 2.0, and Gemini Thinking trailed plain Gemini on SciER ([MimicSFT/R²GRPO](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2505.22068)). Test reasoning only on judgment fields such as edge typing. No 2025–2026 head-to-head of Claude 4.x, GPT-5.x or Gemini 3 with reasoning on versus off was found.

**Context.** Keep each call short and section-routed. In NoLiMa, where the needed fact does not share words with the question, **GPT-4o fell from 99.3% to 69.7% at 32K tokens**, and many models' "effective length" was 2K tokens or less ([NoLiMa](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2502.05167)). Route each field to its sections with code: problem/method from abstract, intro and method; limitations from discussion and limitation sections; edges from intro, method and related work; results from tables plus referencing paragraphs. Nobody has run a controlled comparison of full paper vs section selection on scientific extraction with a frontier model. Run that ablation on your dev set; it is cheap and it would itself be a publishable finding.

**Structured output.** Use strict schema mode, but keep schemas flat. Provider-native structured output reaches **100% compliance on the schemas it accepts**, but OpenAI accepted only **9% of a hard real-world schema set** ([JSONSchemaBench](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.10868v2)). Field order matters. In 2024, forcing JSON cut Claude 3 Haiku's GSM8K accuracy from **86.51 to 23.44**, partly because the model wrote the "answer" key before the "reason" key. Classification-style tasks often *improved* under JSON ([Tam et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2408.02442v1)). Put `evidence_quote` first, then `rationale`, then the label or value. Validate everything with Pydantic and keep a retry path, since even frontier APIs failed JSON parsing about 5% of the time in MOLE ([MOLE](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.19800v1)).

**Evidence grounding.** Do not trust a model's quotes without checking them. On long-context citation, **GPT-4o reached 65.6 and Claude-3-Sonnet 67.2 citation F1**, and GPT-4o's citations averaged about 6 sentences. Asking for citations in the same pass dropped GPT-4o's answer correctness from **78.2 to 69.4** ([LongCite](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2409.02897)). Ask for short, sentence-level quotes. For hard fields, consider a second pass that finds the evidence after the main extraction. Check in layers, cheapest first. First, an exact substring match after whitespace, hyphenation and ligature normalization; this is deterministic and free. Second, a small grounding checker that asks whether the quote supports the claim. **MiniCheck-Flan-T5-L (0.8B) scores 75.0 and Bespoke-MiniCheck-7B 77.4 on LLM-AggreFact, level with Claude-3.5 Sonnet (77.2) and GPT-4o (75.9)**, at roughly 400x lower cost than GPT-4 ([LLM-AggreFact](https://llm-aggrefact.github.io/); [MiniCheck](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.10774v2)). Since no verifier passes about 77%, none should be the only gate. Third, a second model from a different family. Concordant extractions were **0.94 accurate and discordant ones 0.41–0.50**, and cross-critique fixed about half the disagreements ([medRxiv 2024](https://www.medrxiv.org/content/10.1101/2024.09.20.24314108.full.pdf)). That study had only 391 responses, but the routing signal is strong.

**Cost.** Per paper, extraction is cheap. LLMEvalDB processed 2,694 tables for under $500 with a batch API, about **$0.19 per table or $0.29 per paper** ([LLMEvalDB](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.18791v2)). GPT-4.1-Mini processed about 10K papers for **$112, roughly $0.01 per paper** ([arXiv 2510.01235](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.01235v1)). ChatPD's dataset field cost **$0.0006–0.007 per paper** with GPT-4o-mini ([ChatPD](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2505.22349)). MOLE's full 52-paper run cost $1.24 with Gemini 2.5 Pro and $2.60 with Claude 3.5 Sonnet, about 2–5 cents per paper by my arithmetic ([MOLE](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.19800v1)). With about 21 references per paper ([Intern-Atlas](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.28158)), the frontier-model edge typing is the cost driver. A budget of well under $1 per paper is realistic with a mini model for bulk work, frontier escalation only on disagreements, and batch pricing.

**Fine-tuning and distillation.** Distillation is well proven, and it fits your candidate-table design. A T5-11B student trained on 3,434 teacher-labeled tables **matched its teacher (73.2 vs 72.3 F1)** after 380 GPU-hours ([Bai et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.14336)). LongCite-8B, trained on about 45K automatically built examples, **beat GPT-4o on citation F1 (72.0 vs 65.6)** ([LongCite](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2409.02897)). MiniCheck reached GPT-4-level checking with about 14K synthetic examples ([MiniCheck](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.10774v2)). SciTülu 7B matched Tülu V2 70B on science tasks, with gains leveling off around 1,000 examples per task. Mixing in general data prevented forgetting: the general average was 48.9 with the mix vs 23.9 science-only ([SciRIFF](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.07835v4)). For relations, use SFT plus an RL reward rather than plain SFT, because of the out-of-distribution collapse described earlier ([MimicSFT/R²GRPO](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2505.22068)). Your verified, promoted rows become the training set at no extra labeling cost. Fine-tuning a 7–8B model for edge typing is the best path to frontier-level accuracy at open-model cost; ACL-ARC fine-tunes already beat GPT-4 by 25+ points.

## A recommended design: code decides, the LLM fills candidate tables

The design follows from the evidence above. Each LLM call is narrow, section-scoped, and returns a quote or cell ID. Code accepts or rejects. Escalation happens only on disagreement or a failed check. Nothing reaches a canonical table unless `verify/` promotes it.

| Stage | Plain code does (decides) | LLM proposes (into `candidate_*`) | Promotion gate in `verify/` |
|---|---|---|---|
| Section routing | Map sections to fields; build per-field inputs of a few thousand tokens; tag entity spans inline | — | — |
| Artifacts | Regex URLs, footnotes, "code available at"; resolve via GitHub/HF; licence from repo metadata | Label what each link is (code/model/data) and whether it is released by this paper | Link resolves; label is from a fixed enum |
| Card entities | Alias table and identifier linking (arXiv ID, URL, HF/GitHub) | Task/method/dataset/metric mentions with role (proposed/used/compared/mentioned) and quote | Quote is an exact substring; entity links to alias table or goes to the new-entity review queue |
| Card prose fields (problem, method components, author-stated limitations) | Restrict limitations to limitation/discussion sections | One-line field + 1–2 sentence quote, evidence first in the schema | Substring match + grounding checker ≥ threshold; inferred limitations never promoted |
| Results rows | Parse cells with stable IDs; detect numeric cells; header paths; direction (higher/lower better); unit scaling | One record per numeric cell: method, benchmark, split, metric, setting (harness, attempts, base model, shots), with cell ID and a supporting-text quote for settings | Value equals cell text after normalization; benchmark/metric consistent with header path; settings quote verified or field set to "not stated" |
| `evaluates_on`, `compares_against` edges | Derive from verified results rows: a benchmark header or baseline row carrying a citation link | Nothing, or tie-breaking only | Pure rule |
| Other lineage edges (`extends`, `combines`, `replaces_component`, `transplants_domain`, `refutes_or_contradicts`, `uses_artifact`) | Gather every mention of each reference with ±1 sentence and section label; add cited abstract; isInfluential prior; year order | Per reference: type, component, one-line delta, quote. Mini model first; a high-recall pass for rare types | Substring match; year order; no opposite-direction conflict; second-family model agrees, else frontier model or human |

Several details make this work. Classify **per reference**, with all of its mentions grouped by section, rather than per sentence. Each reference has about four functions, so the edge should record the strongest causal one, with its evidence ([MultiCite](https://preview.aclanthology.org/fix_video/2022.naacl-main.137.pdf); [Intern-Atlas](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.28158)). Use Semantic Scholar's isInfluential flag as a cheap "is this lineage at all?" prior. Its underlying model reached **precision about 0.65 at recall 0.90** ([Valenzuela et al.](https://ai2-website.s3.amazonaws.com/publications/ValenzuelaHaMeaningfulCitations.pdf)). Use it to rank references, not to type them. Cache every normalization decision so the LLM never re-decides an alias ([Timmer et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.17465)). Score each stage against its own gold, because errors multiply across stages. SciREX's components scored 0.57–0.71 with gold inputs, yet the full 4-ary pipeline scored **0.008 F1** ([SciREX](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2005.00512)).

## Pitfalls and open gaps worth knowing before you build

The common failure modes are the same across sources:

- **Over-assigning relations to unrelated pairs** ([SciER](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.21155v1)).
- **Counting Related-Work mentions as "used"** ([ChatPD](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2505.22349)).
- **Taking values from the wrong table or from the appendix** ([SciLead](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.12656v1)).
- **Long-context collapse in small models** ([arXiv 2407.02409](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.02409v1)).
- **Citations that are too broad to verify** ([LongCite](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2409.02897)).
- **Low recall hidden behind high precision** in cheap models ([arXiv 2510.01235](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.01235v1)).

Evaluation choices also distort comparisons. Exact vs fuzzy (≥50%) matching, micro vs macro averaging, and closed vs open taxonomies make published numbers incomparable ([Timmer et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.17465); [arXiv 2406.04383](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.04383v2)). Some papers are internally inconsistent. The SOTA? working note gives three different test-set sizes, and its "few-shot/zero-shot" labels name test splits, not prompting styles ([arXiv 2406.04383](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.04383v2)). Several key lineage results (Intern-Atlas, THE-Tree, Sci-Reasoning, R²GRPO) are preprints with self-defined evaluations.

The gaps are just as concrete:

- No benchmark scores a multi-field paper card.
- No benchmark covers typed lineage edges, and no human agreement has been measured for "extends vs replaces vs adapts" distinctions.
- No study reports accuracy for extracting agent harness, scaffold or attempts.
- No study reports the rate of non-verbatim quotes from scientific papers.
- No controlled comparison of full paper vs section context exists for frontier models.
- No current-generation (Claude 4.x / GPT-5.x / Gemini 3) results with reasoning on vs off exist for any of these tasks.

Every one of these can be measured directly from the `candidate_*` tables and the dev/held-out gold your pipeline already plans.

## Conclusion

The literature leaves a gap that your pipeline fills. Published systems either extract richly and verify weakly (Sci-Reasoning, GoR, CoI) or verify well but on narrow, closed taxonomies (AxCell, LLMEvalDB). The one system that does both, Intern-Atlas, uses the same "quote plus deterministic check" pattern as your design. Its 23-point gap between production and audit model shows that verification and selective escalation are what make the graph usable. Your inputs remove the two largest error sources in the older work: PDF table parsing and unlinked citations. So your quality ceiling is set by attribute assignment and edge typing, not by extraction mechanics.

For the thesis, the most useful framing is that **the measurement is the contribution**. A gold set with inter-annotator agreement for your eight edge types and the agent-setting fields would be the first of its kind. So would reporting, per stage, how often LLM proposals fail the deterministic gates. Those rejection rates are a direct, reproducible measure of how far "LLM proposes, code disposes" moves quality beyond what the model achieves alone. The verified rows that pass the gates become a free training set for a small model fine-tuned with RL rewards. That is the most strongly evidenced path to frontier-level edge typing at open-model cost.
