# Check cheaply, merge cautiously, sample randomly

**Short answer:** for the checking step, the best evidence supports a layered gate where each layer is cheaper than the next. First come plain-code rules: the quote is really in the paper, the dates run the right way, the cited paper is in the reference list, and the types fit. Next comes a small grounding checker that asks "does this quote support this templated sentence?" An LLM judge sees only the uncertain band, and a human sees only what is left. Small checkers are as good as frontier LLMs at this job. **MiniCheck-Flan-T5-L (0.8B) scores 75.0 and Bespoke-MiniCheck-7B 77.4 on LLM-AggreFact, against 77.2 for Claude-3.5 Sonnet and 75.9 for GPT-4o**, at roughly 1/400th of GPT-4's cost ([LLM-AggreFact](https://llm-aggrefact.github.io/); [MiniCheck](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2404.10774v2)). But every checker tops out near **59–61 on expert text**, so no single model can be the gate. Entity resolution works best when it is driven by identifiers, with an LLM used only as a narrow adjudicator. ChatPD's identifier graph reached **P 0.98 / R 0.87 for dataset mentions, against F1 0.57 for name matching** ([ChatPD](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.22349v1)). Nobody has published numbers for telling versions apart, such as SWE-bench vs SWE-bench Verified. You need code that treats tokens like "Verified", "Lite", "v2" or "7B" as hard blockers of "same" merges. For your review time, 15–30 minutes a day buys only about **15–40 labels**. So estimate precision with **two-stage sampling (pick a paper, then label 3–5 of its facts)**, which cut annotation time by **up to 60% (static) and 80% (evolving KGs)** ([Gao et al., VLDB 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1907.09657v1)). Keep a fixed random audit share that the router never chooses, and report Wilson intervals. Realistic targets are ±10% per edge type and ±5% for the whole graph.

My own arithmetic (standard binomial formulas, not from a source) gives the sample sizes for a random sample at 95% confidence. At true precision near 90%, **±10% needs 35 labels, ±5% needs 139, and ±3% needs 385**. At 50% the ±5% figure is 385. If 27 of 30 labels are correct, the Wilson interval is **74–97%**. At 90 of 100 it is **83–95%**. If you find zero errors in n labels, the 95% upper bound on the error rate is about 3/n (the "rule of three").

## Grounding checkers match frontier judges, but scientific text caps them near 60–70

The good news is that your checking problem is easier than the one most benchmarks test. LLM-AggreFact asks whether a claim is supported by a whole document. Your rows already carry a verbatim 1–2 sentence quote. That means no chunking and no retrieval, which is the best case for these checkers. MiniCheck handles long documents by cutting them into ~400–500-token chunks and taking the maximum score across chunks ([MiniCheck](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2404.10774v2)). You skip that step. On the general benchmark, the top systems sit within about two points of each other. **A 0.4B DeBERTa (FactCG, 75.6) and a 0.8B Flan-T5 (MiniCheck, 75.0) are effectively tied with GPT-4o (75.9)** ([LLM-AggreFact](https://llm-aggrefact.github.io/)). Bespoke-MiniCheck-7B's lead at 77.4 is a vendor-submitted figure ([Bespoke](https://bespokelabs.ai/bespoke-minicheck)). The bad news is in the expert column. On ExpertQA, every model lands at **59–61** ([LLM-AggreFact](https://llm-aggrefact.github.io/)), and none of the 11 datasets is scientific-paper text. The real accuracy of these checkers on lineage quotes is unknown until you measure it on your dev gold.

Scientific-specific benchmarks point the same way. On MSciNLI, which labels scientific sentence pairs with relation types beyond plain entailment, **the best fine-tuned model reached 77.2 macro-F1 and the best prompted LLM only 51.8** ([MSciNLI](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.08066)). Dedicated claim verifiers also fall apart once the setting gets realistic. MultiVerS drops from **72.5 to 52.4 F1** going from SciFact to the open-retrieval SciFact-Open ([SciFact-Open](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2210.13777)). Prompting tricks help small LLMs a lot: few-shot Mistral-7B rose from **67.3 to 76.0 F1 on SciFact** when first asked to list the facts in the claim ([FactDetect](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.18367v1)). But that table used a different setting from the MultiVerS numbers, so do not compare the two directly. The practical reading is simple. A local fine-tuned checker is the right first gate. An LLM is a second opinion, not the primary judge. Claim decomposition is not worth its cost on short quotes: it moved GPT-4 by +0.3 and MiniCheck by −1.4 at **2–4x the price** ([MiniCheck](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2404.10774v2)).

What works for typed relations is templating plus rules. The two closest published systems both run NLI on a templated hypothesis, and neither publishes its templates or thresholds. SciTraj checks four claim-driven edge types with DeBERTa-v3-MNLI and gates two similarity types with hard rules. Its "direct extension" type needs abstract cosine **≥ 0.92 and a year gap ≤ 2**; its "temporal semantic" type needs **cosine ≥ 0.92 and a gap ≥ 5 years**. Overall edge precision is **79.9% (κ 0.74, n = 300)** ([SciTraj](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.22342)). Per type, precision runs from **87% for direct extension and 85% for limit-addressed down to 63% for disputes**. About **37% of the bad dispute edges were "unlike / in contrast to" phrasing** that signalled a difference in method, not a contradiction ([SciTraj](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.22342)). THE-Tree sends only the cases its NLI model calls neutral to Qwen2.5-72B, and blends the two scores as **0.7·NLI + 0.3·LLM**. It reports **95.6% fact-verification accuracy** and relation **F1 0.70 against expert trees** ([THE-Tree](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.21763v2)). Both are preprints with self-defined evaluations.

For your six edge types, this means one hypothesis template per type. The NLI target must be stated with care. For `refutes_or_contradicts`, the hypothesis is "A reports a result inconsistent with B's claim R", and the quote must *entail* it. A "contradiction" label between quote and hypothesis means the edge is wrong, not that it is a refutation. Add a lexical guard that downgrades quotes whose only signal is "unlike / in contrast to / differs from" to the review queue. `uses_artifact` is really mention detection: a string or ID match of the artifact in the quote plus a registry lookup, with no NLI.

Rules should run before any model, because they remove whole classes of error for free. The useful hard gates are:

- the quote matches the parsed text after normalization;
- the citing paper's arXiv v1 date is not earlier than the cited paper's;
- B is in A's reference list for `extends`, `combines` and `replaces_component`;
- the types fit, so `uses_artifact` must point to an artifact node, `replaces_component` must name a component on B's card, and `transplants_domain` needs two different domains;
- there are no self-loops or duplicates;
- no pair carries both `extends` and `refutes` unless each has its own evidence.

Nobody has measured these rules on their own. The closest ablation is Wikontic's. Removing its whole ontology and typing stage cut the share of ontology-consistent triples from **96.5% to 15.2%** and QA F1 from **55.9 to 48.8** ([Wikontic](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.00590v2)). That stage did more than checking, so the effect is not purely from rules. SciTraj's year-gap rules still let through **9 temporally implausible edges in 300**, mostly with gaps over 10 years ([SciTraj](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.22342)). Your date rule should be a direction check plus a review flag for very long gaps, not a narrow window.

## Calibrated escalation beats picking a threshold by eye

The rigorous way to hit a target precision is the Trust or Escalate method (ICLR 2025). Take about **500 labeled items** and scan thresholds downward from about 0.999. Accept a threshold only if an exact binomial upper bound on its error stays under the target, using fixed-sequence testing. Items below the threshold are escalated, not rejected. With a 90% agreement target, the cascade **met the guarantee in 90.8% of random splits while keeping 55.7% of items**. Naive "pick the threshold where dev precision hits 90%" calibration met it in only **47.5–79.0%** of splits. The heuristic λ = 1 − α met it in **42.0%** ([Trust or Escalate](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.18370v1)). The lesson for a solo researcher is that a threshold picked from a point estimate on a small dev set will overshoot its promised precision about half the time.

The same paper shows how to escalate cheaply. Try a cheap judge first, move to a stronger one when confidence is low, and abstain last. At the 90% target, **56.5% of judgments came from models cheaper than GPT-4**. On ChatArena, **88.1% of kept items were judged by Mistral-7B or GPT-3.5** ([Trust or Escalate](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.18370v1)). The confidence signal matters. Verbalized confidence was nearly useless, with **AUROC 0.548**. "Simulated Annotators" means asking the same model five times with different few-shot examples and using their agreement. It cut GPT-4-turbo's calibration error on TL;DR from **0.196 to 0.039** ([Trust or Escalate](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.18370v1)). Use checker scores and multi-sample agreement as confidence, never the model's own "I am 90% sure."

Agreement between systems helps, but it does not prove anything. In SciFact-Open, claim–abstract pairs flagged by one system were relevant **29%** of the time, and pairs flagged by four were relevant only **60%** ([SciFact-Open](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2210.13777)). The extraction report found a stronger signal for two-family agreement on extraction: about 94% correct when two model families agree, against 41–50% when they disagree ([medRxiv 2024](https://www.medrxiv.org/content/10.1101/2024.09.20.24314108.full.pdf), only 391 responses). Agreement therefore works well as a *routing* signal, but it cannot certify precision on its own. Measure the agreeing bucket's precision on your audit sample like any other stratum.

There is one practical catch. The 500-label calibration set is per decision rule. Your dev gold (60 papers) will not have 500 examples of `transplants_domain` or `refutes_or_contradicts`. For common types (`uses_artifact`, `extends`), calibrate per type. For rare types, either pool them under one conservative threshold or skip auto-promotion and send everything to review. Calibrate on `data/gold/dev` only. The held-out 40 papers are for `eval/` to report final precision, and your hard rules forbid tuning on them anyway.

## Identifiers and version blockers beat embeddings for entity resolution

Production scholarly graphs merge with simple, mostly deterministic rules, and few of them publish how often they merge wrongly. CS-KG lemmatizes names, expands acronyms that are defined in brackets, and merges entities that share a token when their embedding cosine is **≥ 0.9**. It keeps the merged name as an alternative label ([CS-KG](https://boa.unimib.it/retrieve/87e76150-08c7-4db6-83d3-e3332fadac7a/Dessi-2022-Lect%20Notes%20Computer%20Sci-preprint.pdf)). It reports no merge precision. OpenAlex matches works by DOI and metadata and disambiguates authors with ML, with no published accuracy ([OpenAlex](https://help.openalex.org/data/how-its-built/)). Two systems do publish numbers, and they show the risk. OpenAIRE's paper deduplication had **about 23% false merges** in an expert check of 1,200 entries ([IRCDL 2022](https://ceur-ws.org/Vol-3160/short16.pdf)). ORKG estimates that **up to 30% of its predicates may be redundant**, and blames user behaviour and interface design ([John et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.03197)). False merges are the costlier error. They spread wrong facts across every edge that touches the merged node, and S2AND added hard rules against them because "such errors hurt user trust" ([S2AND](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2103.07534)).

For ML methods, datasets and benchmarks, the strongest result comes from identifiers rather than names. ChatPD links each dataset description to "identity nodes", one per unique name or URL. It matches a description when the two share a node, then fills in missing identifiers for up to 3 rounds. **It deletes any identifier that points to more than one entity**, such as a generic kaggle.com URL. It creates a new entity only when a mention has a name, a specific URL and appears in **≥ 3 papers**. The result is **P 0.983 / R 0.873 / F1 0.883**, against **F1 0.568 for plain name matching** and 0.630 for the unsupervised ZeroER ([ChatPD](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.22349v1)). Expect about **half of extracted dataset mentions to have no catalog entry**, so the new-entity rule is not optional ([ChatPD](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.22349v1)). The LLM is useful one step later, as a classifier over candidates. MapperGPT uses a high-recall lexical matcher, then asks GPT-4 to label each pair EXACT / BROADER / NARROWER / RELATED / DIFFERENT. That lifted F1 from **0.340 to 0.672**. Recall was capped by the candidate generator, which missed 10–20% of true mappings ([MapperGPT](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2310.03666)). That five-way label set maps directly onto your needs: same, variant/subset, broader, related, different. Generic entity-matching studies add that **there is no single best prompt**, so prompts need tuning for each model and dataset ([Peeters et al., EDBT 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.11244v4)).

Versions and variants are where nobody has done the work. **No benchmark or reported number exists for resolving ML model or benchmark versions.** The closest resource is SciER's SYNONYM-OF / SUBCLASS-OF / PART-OF labels. Even there, relation extraction peaks at **61.1 F1 supervised and 41.2 for an LLM** ([SciER](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2410.21155)). Embedding similarity is close to its maximum for exactly the pairs you most need to keep apart, such as "SWE-bench" vs "SWE-bench Lite". A CS-KG-style cosine ≥ 0.9 merge would silently collapse them. Sci-ZSEL finds that LLM-generated aliases "semantically drift toward ontology neighbors", and that curated synonyms plus filtered LLM aliases work best ([Sci-ZSEL](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.00228)). The fix borrows S2AND's idea of blocking incompatible first names. A deterministic parser pulls variant markers out of each name: Verified, Lite, Full, v1/v2, 1k/22k, 7B/70B, -Instruct, -mini, split names, harness names. Two names that differ by any marker can never be labelled "same". They default to `variant_of` or `subset_of`, and the LLM may only choose among those. Seed the alias table from the frozen Papers with Code archive (shut down July 2025; peak about **9,327 benchmarks and 5,628 datasets**) ([Codesota](https://www.codesota.com/papers-with-code)), knowing that it misses everything after 2025.

Papers, authors and repos are easier because they carry identifiers. For papers, deterministic keys are enough: DOI, arXiv ID with the version suffix stripped, and normalized title + year + first-author surname. The main hard case is an arXiv preprint against its published version, which should become a `version_of` link rather than a merge. For authors, do not build your own system. S2AND's blocking + LightGBM + average-linkage clustering reached **B³ F1 0.900 against 0.784 for the old Semantic Scholar system**, more than halving errors ([S2AND](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2103.07534)). Take Semantic Scholar or OpenAlex author IDs as given. For repos, link through explicit identifiers: a GitHub URL in the paper, or an arXiv ID in a README or Hugging Face model card ([HF Paper Pages](https://huggingface.co/docs/hub/main/paper-pages)). No reliable accuracy figure for learned paper-to-repo linkers was found.

Reversibility is a design choice, not something the literature measures. Never delete a merged entity. Store each merge as an event row with its evidence, the rule or model that made it, `recorded_at` and `superseded_at`. Resolve canonical IDs through a redirect table. An unmerge then supersedes the event, and the old surface forms survive as alternative labels, as in CS-KG. Cache every LLM adjudication by (pair, prompt_id, model) so a rebuild replays the same decision. LLM matchers give different answers from run to run ([MapperGPT](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2310.03666)). After each batch, apply ChatPD's ambiguity rule continuously: alert when one identifier starts mapping to two canonical entities, or when a cluster's size jumps. Report cluster quality with **B³ F1 plus false merges and false splits counted separately**, as S2AND does ([S2AND](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2103.07534)).

## Fifteen to forty labels a day forces cluster sampling and a fixed audit share

Your daily review budget is the binding constraint, so the arithmetic comes first. Gao et al.'s measured timings work out to about **45–60 seconds per triple**, including looking up the entity. For example, 1.85 hours bought 149 NELL triples ([Gao et al.](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10112928)). At that pace, 15–30 minutes buys **about 15–40 labels a day**. A lineage edge that needs reading a citation context will be slower, and a card fact with its quote beside it may be faster. One ±5% estimate for one edge type at 90% precision (139 labels) takes about **4–9 review days**. Estimating all eight-plus fact families to ±5% every month is not possible. The realistic plan is **±10% per type (35–62 labels each) and ±5% for the pooled, weighted graph**.

Two-stage cluster sampling is what makes the budget stretch. Sample a paper with probability proportional to its number of new facts, then label 3–5 of its facts while the paper is already loaded in your head. In Gao et al.'s experiments this cut human time by **up to 60% on static KGs and 80% on evolving ones**. Cost fell as the number of facts per paper rose to about 5, then levelled off or rose. Adding strata on top saved up to another 40%. On NELL, time fell from 1.85 h to 1.04 h with good strata ([Gao et al.](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10112928)). The cleverer KGEval method passes labels along through constraints between facts and needed **140 queries against 623 for random sampling** on NELL ([KGEval](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1610.06912)). But it cost *more* human time in Gao's measurements, **3.17 h vs 0.44 h** on YAGO, because it jumps between entities. Stratify by fact family (edge type, card field, merge) and by verifier outcome. Report a Wilson interval per stratum plus a weighted overall interval. Gao et al. use the normal approximation, which is too narrow when n is under ~100 or precision is above 90%. That describes most of your strata.

The trap is mixing two jobs in one queue. Routing uncertain items to yourself is the right way to *fix* errors and improve prompts. But it biases any precision estimate, because you see more errors than exist in the graph. Split the daily budget. About **60% goes to a random or stratified audit that the router never touches**, and only that share feeds the precision number. The other 40% goes to the routed queue, which is excluded from the estimate unless you apply inverse-probability weights. This split is my design inference. The evidence that routing alone cannot cover the work comes from Trust or Escalate: at strict targets, **20–45% of items escalate** ([Trust or Escalate](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2407.18370)). At hundreds of new facts a day, that far exceeds 40 labels. So escalated items must be allowed to *wait* in `candidate_*` with a "pending" status rather than block the pipeline. Order the routed queue by blast radius: entity merges first (one bad merge corrupts many edges), then lineage edges that feed analytics, then card facts.

Pre-filling the label is worth it, but it needs a control. In a controlled study, pre-annotation made annotators **1.7x faster with unchanged accuracy (LAS 95.0 vs 94.5)**, and agreement rose to κ 0.99 vs 0.96. The authors note that judgments were "influenced by the output" of the pre-annotator ([Mikulová et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2306.09307)). That pre-annotator was strong (~91% accurate). A weaker or systematically wrong LLM makes anchoring riskier. Annotators who were more skeptical of AI caught more errors. Annotators were less likely to fix a label when the fix required typing a new value ([Beck, LMU 2025](https://edoc.ub.uni-muenchen.de/36241/)). In your review UI, hide the proposed label on a random **10–20% of audit items**, and compare blind vs shown precision to *measure* anchoring. Make reject one keystroke, and always show the quote beside the claim.

Gold-set construction follows the citation-function playbook. Jurgens et al. built ACL-ARC from a **10-paper pilot**, then two rounds of guideline refinement, then two expert annotators with adjudication. They oversampled rare classes by keyword, since FUTURE, EXTENSION and MOTIVATION had only **68–98 examples against 1,021 BACKGROUND** ([Jurgens et al., TACL 2018](https://aclanthology.org/Q18-1028.pdf)). SciCite chose 3 coarse classes precisely because they gave "high inter-annotator agreement", and its expert check showed **86% agreement with crowd labels** ([SciCite](https://alphaxiv.org/abs/1904.01608)). With **about 27 citations per paper** in ACL-ARC, 100 gold papers would mean ~2,700 citation contexts. That is too many for one person.

My estimate (derived, not sourced) is that at 45–60 seconds per fact, 100 papers with ~20 facts each is about **25–35 hours**, or **2–4 months** at your daily budget. So annotate fully the card fields, the merges those papers touch, and only the lineage-relevant citations: those to papers already in the graph, or those in the method and experiment sections. Split dev and held-out *by paper*, not by fact. Stratify by year and venue, and oversample rare types into both splits. As a solo annotator you have no second human, so measure **intra-annotator agreement**: re-label 10–20% of dev papers blind after at least two weeks. An LLM can flag disagreements for you to adjudicate. Do not report LLM–human κ as inter-annotator agreement. If a type's self-agreement is poor in the pilot, merge it into a coarser class, as SciCite did.

For daily monitoring, treat each ingestion batch as a stratum. Gao et al.'s stratified incremental evaluation cost **about 50% less than reservoir-based re-evaluation, and 20–67% less** as update accuracy varied ([Gao et al.](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10112928)). Label 5–10 audit items per batch and keep a rolling 4-week weighted precision with a Wilson interval. Alert when its lower bound falls below target. Several leading indicators cost no labels: verifier rejection rate, two-model disagreement rate, per-type edge volume, merge-rate spikes, and identifiers that map to two entities. Re-run the held-out eval after every prompt, model or threshold change, not on a calendar. Knowledge Vault's core idea, calibrated per-fact probabilities, is the right goal ([Google Research](https://research.google/pubs/knowledge-vault-a-web-scale-approach-to-probabilistic-knowledge-fusion/)). Check it by bucketing confidence into bands and comparing each band's predicted precision with labeled precision.

On tooling, a thin custom review page or CLI over `candidate_*` fits your rules best. It writes accept/reject/edit, a blind flag, the stratum and the sampling probability into a `review_labels` table. Generic tools handle the audit-vs-routed split, blind items and cluster sampling poorly. For annotating gold from scratch, INCEpTION and Label Studio Community are free and self-hosted, and Prodigy (~$490 one-time, free academic licences) is the fastest single-user option. Argilla needs Elasticsearch, Postgres and Redis, and reportedly has had no release since March 2025 ([Potato comparison](https://potatoannotator.readthedocs.io/en/latest/comparison/), vendor-authored).

## A recommended design: rules gate, checkers score, LLMs adjudicate, you audit

The design below puts the evidence into your layout. Every LLM call goes through `llm.call` and writes only to `candidate_*`. Every promotion is a deterministic decision in `verify/`. Every threshold is a config value calibrated on dev gold.

| Stage | Code decides | Model proposes (to `candidate_*`) | Promotion rule in `verify/` | Starting threshold |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0. Hard rules | Quote substring after normalization; date direction; B in A's references; type constraints; no self-loops or duplicates; no extends+refutes without separate evidence | — | Any failure → reject (kept with reason code) | Binary |
| 1. Deterministic types | `uses_artifact`, `evaluates_on`, `compares_against` from ID/string match and verified result rows | — | Pure rule | Binary |

| ER: new entity | Needs name + specific URL + ≥3 papers | — | Rule | λ = 3 |
| ER: storage | Merge events with `recorded_at` / `superseded_at`, redirect table, cached adjudications | — | Unmerge = supersede | — |

There are two deliberate asymmetries. First, nothing is *rejected* just for being uncertain. It stays in `candidate_*` as pending, so recall is never lost to a busy week. Second, entity merges have a higher bar than edges, because one false merge corrupts every edge that touches it.

## Pitfalls and gaps to plan around

The pitfalls the sources document are concrete. Checkers look strong on general benchmarks and then reach about 60 on expert text. "Contrast" phrasing gets mistaken for refutation, which was 37% of SciTraj's dispute errors. Thresholds picked from point estimates miss their promised precision about half the time. Verbalized LLM confidence is close to useless (AUROC 0.548). High-cosine merges collapse versions. Generic URLs link unrelated datasets. LLM aliases drift toward neighbouring entities. Production deduplication can run about 23% false merges. Uncertainty-routed review inflates your error estimate if you mix it into precision. Pre-filled labels anchor reviewers. Several key systems (SciTraj, THE-Tree, ChatPD, Wikontic) are preprints with self-defined evaluations. Bespoke-MiniCheck's leaderboard entry is vendor-submitted.

The gaps are just as concrete:

- No benchmark verifies claims against scientific-paper evidence quotes in LLM-AggreFact style.
- Neither SciTraj nor THE-Tree publishes its templates or NLI thresholds.
- No per-type precision exists for "replaces component" or "transplants domain" relations.
- No benchmark or number exists for resolving ML model or benchmark versions.
- No accuracy figure was found for Splink or dedupe on bibliographic data, or for paper-to-repo linkers.
- No primary label-savings figure for active learning in relation extraction could be verified.
- No timing study exists for reviewing scientific lineage edges.
- No source quantifies how often production systems reverse merges.

Each of these can be measured from your own `review_labels` and gold tables. Your pipeline will produce that data as a side effect, so each gap is a contribution open to your thesis.

## Conclusion

The research changes where the effort should go. Choosing a verifier model is nearly a solved, cheap decision: any of the 0.4–8B checkers is as good as a frontier judge, and none is good enough alone. The real work sits in three places the literature leaves open. The first is per-type hypothesis templates with lexical guards. The second is a variant-marker parser that stops version collapse. The third is a sampling design that gives honest precision from about 30 labels a day. All three are deterministic code, which fits "LLM proposes, deterministic code disposes" well.

The most useful reframing is to treat the review budget as a *measurement instrument* rather than a cleanup crew. With a fixed random audit share, blind items and cluster sampling, the same 20 minutes a day yields a defensible precision estimate, an anchoring measurement and calibration data for the next threshold. No published system reports all three for a lineage graph. Done this way, the checking step is not only quality control for the graph. It is the evaluation section of the thesis, gathered as you go.
